Logarithms & Indices

Indices, Just Asked Backwards

LC Maths · Ordinary & Higher Level · Laws of indices · laws of logarithms · solving exponential equations

Logarithms get treated like a brand new, alien topic. They're not. Every log law on the syllabus is a law of indices you already know, just looked at from the other direction. Once you see indices and logs as the same idea asked two different ways, the "rules to memorise" mostly disappear — they're just facts about indices, restated.

1. The question indices can't answer

Indices are shorthand for repeated multiplication: \(a^n\) means \(a\) multiplied by itself \(n\) times. That's an easy question to answer going forward — work out \(2^5\) and you get \(32\), no problem. But flip the question around: "2 to what power gives 32?" Indices don't answer that. They only ever state the forward direction. A logarithm is simply the name for the backward question: \(\log_a b\) means "the power you raise \(a\) to, to get \(b\)." Formally,

\[ \log_a b = c \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad a^c = b \]

That's the whole definition. Nothing about a logarithm is more mysterious than "what's the missing exponent?"

2. Indices turn multiplication into addition

Here's the idea everything else in this post hangs off. Multiply two powers of the same base and the exponents add:

\[ a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n} \]

That's not a coincidence to memorise — it falls straight out of what indices mean: \(a^m\) is \(m\) copies of \(a\) multiplied together, \(a^n\) is \(n\) more copies, so \(a^m \times a^n\) is \(m+n\) copies altogether. What matters is what this does: it sets up a hidden correspondence between two number lines — the actual values (which combine by multiplying) and their exponents (which combine by adding). Multiplying on one line is exactly addition on the other.

3. Logs are indices, asked backwards

Since \(\log_a b\) just means "the exponent that gives \(b\)," converting between index form and log form is the same statement written two ways — nothing is calculated, you're just relabelling.

Worked algebra — converting and evaluating

Index form to log form: \(2^5=32\) says exactly the same thing as \(\log_2 32 = 5\).

Evaluating a log is asking the missing-exponent question directly: to find \(\log_3 81\), ask "3 to what power gives 81?" Since \(3^4=81\),

\[ \log_3 81 = 4 \]

4. Where the multiplication law comes from

The most-used log law, \(\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y\), isn't a separate fact to learn — it's section 2's index law, \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\), just read off in exponent language instead of value language.

Worked proof — the multiplication law

Let \(m = \log_a x\) and \(n = \log_a y\), so by definition \(x = a^m\) and \(y = a^n\).

Multiply \(x\) and \(y\) together and use the index law from section 2:

\[ xy = a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n} \]

Now take \(\log_a\) of both sides. By definition, \(\log_a\) of \(a\) raised to something just returns that exponent, so the right-hand side gives \(m+n\):

\[ \log_a(xy) = m+n = \log_a x + \log_a y \]

The log law is the index law — the same fact, just extracting the exponent instead of the value.

5. The division and power laws come from the same place

The other two log laws fall out exactly the same way, starting from the matching index law instead.

Worked algebra — division and power laws

Dividing powers subtracts exponents, \(a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}\). With \(x=a^m,\ y=a^n\) as before:

\[ \frac{x}{y} = a^{m-n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log_a\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = m-n = \log_a x - \log_a y \]

Raising a power to a power multiplies exponents, \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\). With \(x=a^m\):

\[ x^n = (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log_a(x^n) = mn = n\log_a x \]

6. Logs and exponentials are mirror images

Because a logarithm is exponentiation run backwards, the graphs of \(y=a^x\) and \(y=\log_a x\) are reflections of each other across the line \(y=x\) — the standard picture for any function and its inverse. Wherever the exponential passes through \((p,q)\), the logarithm passes through \((q,p)\).

7. Putting it to work: solving with logs

This is the payoff — the question from section 1, actually answered. An equation like \(3^x=20\) has no index-law shortcut: 20 isn't a whole power of 3. Logs solve it directly.

Worked example — solve \(3^x = 20\)

Take \(\log\) of both sides (any base works; base 10 is convenient):

\[ \log(3^x) = \log 20 \]

Use the power law from section 5 to bring the exponent down:

\[ x\log 3 = \log 20 \]

\[ x = \frac{\log 20}{\log 3} \approx 2.727 \]

8. Where this shows up in real life

Anywhere a quantity varies over an enormous multiplicative range, scientists reach for a log scale — for exactly the reason in section 2: it turns huge multiplicative differences into small, manageable additive ones. Earthquake magnitude (the Richter scale) is a \(\log_{10}\) scale of shaking amplitude, so each whole number up is \(\times10\) the amplitude:

\[ \text{magnitude 7 vs. magnitude 5} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 10^{7-5} = 100 \text{ times the amplitude} \]

not "2/5 harder," as the raw numbers might suggest. Chemistry's pH scale (\(\text{pH}=-\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]\)) and sound's decibel scale work the same way — each built so a small additive step stands in for a large multiplicative one.

The other direction — section 7's technique — shows up whenever something grows or shrinks by a fixed percentage each period: compound interest, population growth, radioactive decay. These all follow the same shape as \(3^x=20\), just with a real formula in place of \(3^x\), and solving for time always means taking logs.

Worked example — how long to double your money

Compound interest at rate \(r\) per period gives \(A = P(1+r)^t\) after \(t\) periods. Doubling means \(A=2P\), so:

\[ (1+r)^t = 2 \]

Take logs and use the power law, exactly as in section 7:

\[ t\log(1+r) = \log 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \frac{\log 2}{\log(1+r)} \]

At \(r=5\%\) per year (\(1+r=1.05\)):

\[ t = \frac{\log 2}{\log 1.05} \approx 14.2 \text{ years} \]

Pictures help you remember. They don't replace practice.

The two number lines and the mirror-image graph are here so the log laws stop feeling like a separate list to memorise — they're the index laws you already knew, read the other way. They're not a substitute for practice, though: you still need reps converting between forms, applying each law, and solving log and exponential equations under exam conditions.
